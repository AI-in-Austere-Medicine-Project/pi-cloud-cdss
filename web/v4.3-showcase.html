Where the Answer
Came From

EdgeCDSS 4.3, live on a Jetson in Tanzania — four screenshots, three sources of truth.

Version 4.3.0 · Four unedited responses · Captured August 2026

What you are looking at

EdgeCDSS runs on a single small computer that travels with the team. No hospital network, no cloud dependency for the parts that matter, no assumption that anyone is reachable.

Below are four real responses from it, unedited. They were chosen because each one shows a different part of how the system decides what it may tell a clinician — and, in two of them, how it says so when it cannot properly source an answer.

If you want the engineering detail behind any of this, it is in the version 4.3 release notes and in the repository. There is a live portal, but it runs on one machine in the field and may be offline when you try it.

1. A drug bundle, and why you can check it

A medic types one line: an 80 kg patient, rapid sequence intubation, ketamine and rocuronium. What comes back is not the language model doing arithmetic. The model reads the question and works out what is being asked. The doses themselves come out of a bank of entries that a clinician reviewed and signed, and each entry carries the guideline it came from — the cautions on this screen cite JTS ID39, the military rapid sequence intubation pathway.

The volumes work the same way, with one addition. Rocuronium is stocked at a single strength, so the system prints 9.6 mL of 10 mg/mL — the volume and the concentration it assumed, together, so the medic can hold the ampoule up and check that the two agree. Ketamine ships in several strengths. Rather than pick one and hope, the system withholds the volume and asks which vial is in hand.

The milligram dose is stated either way. 160 mg is 160 mg whether or not anyone confirms the vial, and the screen says so in as many words.

2. The dose it will not give

The same system, asked for push-dose epinephrine in a patient who is shocked but undifferentiated. The signed epinephrine entry covers one indication: symptomatic bradycardia. It does not cover shock.

So the system does not produce a number. It says why — that the entries it holds are for a different indication at different doses — and it points to local protocol, or to an infusion if that is what the patient actually needs. It still explains how to mix the 10 mcg/mL preparation, because that is a mixing instruction rather than a dose. What it will not do is take a figure that was signed off for bradycardia and quietly serve it for shock.

This is the part worth sitting with. A number the system cannot source is not a weaker answer that arrives with a caveat attached. It is not an answer, and it is left out. Most clinical AI will produce something here. This behaviour is deliberate.

3. Ventilator settings, written by a clinician

Ventilator guidance does not come from the guideline library at all. It comes from cards that a clinician wrote, dated and signed, and the system serves the card as written. It is not searching for the answer and it is not composing one.

The cards are filed by physiology rather than by diagnosis, and the heading on this one says so: metabolic acidosis, compensation preserved. That distinction is the whole point. The diagnosis is not what sets the rate. A patient in diabetic ketoacidosis is breathing fast to hold their pH up, and the card's instruction is to match that — start at 20 to 30 and titrate — because a normal rate halves their minute ventilation and the pH falls. A patient with ARDS-pattern lungs needs the opposite handling, and rather than stretch to cover both, this card states plainly that it does not apply to them and names the card that does.

If nobody has signed a card, the system treats it as though it does not exist. There is no override. Before 4.3, this exact question — intubated DKA patient, what do I set — returned no settings at all. That gap is why the module was built.

4. A question the guidelines do not answer

EdgeCDSS was built for trauma. Its guideline library is the Joint Trauma System's practice guidelines, and tropical medicine is not in it. Severe malaria is an entirely reasonable thing to ask in Tanzania, and the library has no chapter on it.

The answer it gives is sound enough. What matters is how it is presented. The system prints the passages it actually matched and how closely they matched: sepsis in prolonged field care at 43 per cent, a chapter on biological casualties at 33 per cent. Those are the nearest things the trauma library holds and they are visibly not malaria guidance. The response is marked NEEDS_HUMAN_REVIEW and carries a note to use local protocol and medical control where available.

The timing says the same thing. The drug bundle took 41 milliseconds and the ventilator card took 4, because both were assembled by ordinary software out of signed material. This one took 5.7 seconds and is attributed to the language model, because that is what had to answer it.

Across these four screens there are three different things answering: signed dose entries, a clinician-authored card, and — as on the epinephrine screen, whose source line reads General Evidence-Based Medicine — general reference from outside the library. The claim is not that one of them is better. It is that the answer always tells you which one it was.

The through-line

Every answer on this page names where it came from, and the system declines when it cannot say. Those are the same property seen from two sides. A system that will tell you its source is a system that has to admit when it hasn't got one.

If you are a clinician, we want you to break it

The people this is built for work in prehospital, retrieval, tactical and austere medicine. If that is you, the failures you find are worth considerably more to us than the successes. A wrong dose, a confident answer to a question it should have declined, a card that reads well and is clinically wrong in the field — those are the findings we need, and they are the ones that are hardest to generate from a desk.

Open an issue on GitHub with what you asked and what came back. The live portal is the quickest way to try it, with the standing caveat that it runs on one machine in the field and may be offline.
